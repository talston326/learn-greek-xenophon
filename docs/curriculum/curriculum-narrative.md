# Curriculum Narrative

**Module 1: σοφία — Wisdom and Socrates**  
**Scope:** Unit 0 and Lessons 1–24
**Updated:** 11 October 2026

This is the project's standing narrative plan for *Learn Greek with Xenophon*. Ask Codex to **open “Curriculum Narrative”** to revisit or revise it. Unit 0 and Lessons 1–12 describe authored course material; the Module II section below plans Lessons 13–24. This document records curriculum intent and source boundaries. It does not itself publish or replace lesson content on the website.

## Narrative arc

Students first learn to read Greek, then meet a young Xenophon in his household and school. His father's departure gives him an early question about becoming useful to Athens. Socrates' unexpected question in Lesson 5 draws him from the household into the wider civic world. Later readings explore physical training, pilgrimage, work, and friendship before Proxenus' invitation leads Xenophon to Delphi and, ultimately, toward Cyrus' expedition. Module 2 can begin with the gathering army and the March of the Ten Thousand.

## Existing course: Unit 0 and Lessons 1–5

### Unit 0 — Greek Alphabet & Reading Readiness

**Role in the story:** The entryway to the narrative rather than a Xenophon reading. Students learn the Greek letters and sounds so they can begin reading the lessons that follow. The course calls this **Unit 0**, not “Lesson 0.”

**Language work:** Uppercase and lowercase letters, final sigma, pronunciation, diphthongs, breathings, accents, keyboarding, and early decoding. See the current [course outline](../../script.js) and [grammar progression](grammar-progression-48-lessons.md).

### Lesson 1 — Xenophon at Home

Young Xenophon lives in Erchia with his father Gryllus and his mother. He tends the family's horse, carries water and grain, and helps before coming inside for dinner. This first reading introduces care, work, and the relationship between father and son. **The childhood scene is a course reconstruction, not a documented incident.**

**Language work:** Present active verbs, singular nominative and accusative, articles, and basic noun–adjective agreement. **Current source:** [Lesson 1 course content](../../lesson-data.js).

### Lesson 2 — The Household of Xenophon

The view widens from Xenophon and the horse to the whole household. Gryllus works in the field; Xenophon's mother directs household work; the women of the household weave and prepare bread. Xenophon helps with the horse, donkey, water, firewood, and garden. The reading presents many kinds of labor while establishing the setting from which Xenophon will later depart. **These scenes are plausible reconstructions, not records of Xenophon's childhood.**

**Language work:** Second-declension nouns, adjective agreement, possessive genitives, forms of εἰμί, and simple prepositions. **Current source:** [Lesson 2 reading migration](../../db/migrations/0013_replace_lesson_2_household_reading.sql).

### Lesson 3 — The Education of Xenophon

Xenophon divides his day between chores and school. After caring for the animals and helping at home, he goes with an attendant to a teacher, learns letters, hears Homer, and encounters music. His desire to learn becomes more explicit, preparing for his meeting with Socrates. **The household and school scenes are reconstructions for beginning Greek.**

**Language work in the current lesson:** Third-person present verbs, direct objects, infinitives, introductory middle/passive forms, and demonstratives. Some forms appear as supported reading exposure ahead of the tighter proposed grammar sequence. **Current source:** [Lesson 3 reading migration](../../db/migrations/0014_replace_lesson_3_education_reading.sql).

### Lesson 4 — Gryllus Rides to War

Gryllus prepares his horse and equipment before leaving for war. Xenophon helps groom the horse and clean the armor; his mother prepares food and bids Gryllus farewell. The parting leaves Xenophon thinking about how he might someday serve Athens. **This particular departure is a course reconstruction, not a documented childhood event.** The culture page adds historical context about Athenian cavalry.

**Language work:** Singular noun cases, adjective agreement, case functions, and accent foundations. **Current sources:** [Lesson 4 reading migration](../../db/migrations/0015_replace_lesson_4_reading.sql), [language-study alignment](lesson-4-language-study-alignment.md), and [culture-page notes](lesson-4-culture-quiz.md).

### Lesson 5 — An Unexpected Question

On his way to buy bread, Xenophon meets Socrates in a narrow lane. Socrates asks where to find ordinary goods, then asks where people become good and honorable. Xenophon cannot answer. A bread seller and Socrates' companions give the adapted scene warmth and humor; Xenophon leaves with Socrates, ready to learn. The core meeting comes from a **later ancient anecdote in [Diogenes Laertius 2.48](https://www.hs-augsburg.de/~harsch/graeca/Chronologia/S_ante04/Xenophon/xen_diog.html)**; the bread seller and much of the dialogue are course inventions. Page 3, **“The Athens Agora,”** situates their encounter in the civic and commercial space and includes a plan of the Classical Agora.

**Language work:** Third-person plurals, commands, infinitives, and review of singular persons and proclitics. **Current sources:** [Lesson 5 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-5.json) and [reading plan](lesson-5-first-meeting.md).

## Continuing readings: Lessons 6–12

Lessons 6–12 have three authored pages each, including Language Study and culture.

### Lesson 6 — Strength of Body and Mind

At a gymnasium or palaestra, **Xenophon** and **Clinias (Κλεινίας)** stand near the seated **Socrates** and **Epigenes**. Epigenes has a slighter frame than the other young men. The Greek narrative reading should name all four men (Ξενοφῶν, Κλεινίας, Σωκράτης, Ἐπιγένης, inflected as needed), so the cast in the text matches the banner. In the courtyard behind them, adult athletes practice wrestling and pankration without clothing; the image keeps their lower bodies obscured. The contests are background action. Socrates notices that Epigenes is out of condition and presses him to train his body as seriously as his mind. The question for the reading is why fitness matters to a citizen even if he is not a professional athlete. Socrates' advice to Epigenes is recorded in [Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 3.12](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Mem.+3.12&fromdoc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0208). The source does **not** place Xenophon or Clinias at this exchange, name a gymnasium as its setting, or describe these particular contests; those details are course reconstruction.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 6 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-6.json) follows this scene and names all four characters. The reading uses the gymnasium training to introduce οἱ φίλοι, τοὺς φίλους, τῶν φίλων, and τοῖς φίλοις before Socrates speaks to Epigenes. Page 2 teaches plural cases, adjective agreement, and shifting accents, with topic practice and final grammar exercises. Page 3 explains the gymnasium with a credited photograph of Olympia's palaestra and a final quiz.

**Language target:** Plural cases and shifting accents. Make groups of athletes, exercises, and objects do useful work in the story; gloss any forms outside the lesson target.

### Lesson 7 — The Road to Eleusis

Xenophon and Clinias join the procession through the Sacred Gate and onto the Sacred Way toward Eleusis. Myrrhine, a fictional Athenian woman, explains why she honors Demeter, guides her sister, and watches the long road. The four travelers arrive before the sanctuary entrance, with the Telesterion beyond. The story of Demeter and Kore adds anticipation, and the reading ends before the sanctuary's private rites. The public procession along the Sacred Way and the participation of women are documented in the [Metropolitan Museum of Art's account](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/mystery-cults-in-the-greek-and-roman-world). **Xenophon's own attendance is not attested**; the characters' journey and conversation are course reconstructions.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 7 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-7.json) includes vocabulary, a seven-paragraph reading with translation and glosses, word study, four grammar sections with topic practice, final grammar exercises, matching road and arrival illustrations, a licensed sanctuary plan on the culture page, Eleusis's significance to Athenians and other Greeks, and a final quiz.

**Language target:** All six persons of the regular present active indicative, noun and adjective agreement, and first-declension feminine -η and long -α forms. The fixed name Ἱερὰ Ὁδός and irregular proper names are glossed reading exposure, not production targets.

### Lesson 8 — A Household Finds a Way

After civil strife, Aristarchus' sisters, nieces, and cousins face the problem of supporting a crowded household. They assess their skills and organize textile work, with meaningful choices and dialogue of their own. The situation and Socrates' counsel to Aristarchus come from [*Memorabilia* 2.7](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0208%3Abook%3D2). Xenophon does not preserve the women's individual words; those voices would be a labeled reconstruction. This reading gives the module a view of women's work and agency outside Xenophon's childhood household.

**Language target:** Declension variants, adjectives, and adverbs for describing people, materials, work, and results.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 8 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-8.json) has an eight-paragraph source-based reading with reconstructed women’s voices, word study, five grammar topics, topic practice, required grammar exercises, a culture page with archaeological context, and a final quiz. The [publication migration](../../db/migrations/0027_publish_lesson_8.sql) prepares the database-backed version; local files alone do not publish it. The main banner uses an open left area so the title does not cover the woman carrying wool.

### Lesson 9 — What Makes a Good Friend?

Socrates asks a small group what makes someone a good friend. A proposed cast is **Plato, Antisthenes, and Critobulus**, with Xenophon listening and contributing. Let them disagree briefly about loyalty, usefulness, and character. Socrates' conversation with Critobulus about friendship in [*Memorabilia* 2.6](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Mem.+2.6&lang=original) provides the central question; [Xenophon's *Symposium* 1.3–4](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/passage/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0032.tlg004.perseus-eng2%3A1.3-1.4/) establishes Antisthenes and Critobulus among Socrates' companions. **No ancient source attests this joint conversation with Plato and Xenophon.** It should be presented as a source-informed fictional discussion, not a lost historical scene.

**Language target:** Alpha-contract verbs and elision in short, natural exchanges. Keep the philosophical vocabulary small and supported by glosses.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 9 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-9.json) includes an eight-paragraph dialogue, blue glosses, word study, five grammar topics, practice rounds, required grammar exercises, a symposium culture page, and a final quiz. The [publication migration](../../db/migrations/0029_publish_lesson_9.sql) prepares the database-backed version. The culture page reproduces a licensed photograph of a symposium fresco from the Tomb of the Diver at Paestum; the lesson banner illustrates the proposed group in an Athenian home.

### Lesson 10 — The Letter from Proxenus

Proxenus, Xenophon's old friend, invites him to join Cyrus. The offer is tempting, but it may endanger Xenophon's standing in Athens. Xenophon shows the letter to Socrates, who urges him to consult Apollo at Delphi. These events come from [*Anabasis* 3.1.4–5](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0032%2C006%3A3); any full text of the letter or extended conversation would be adapted dialogue.

**Language target:** Pronouns, possession, and adjective placement: *my friend*, *your journey*, *this decision*. The reading should turn the grammar into a personal dilemma.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 10 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-10.json) has a developed eight-paragraph reading that names Proxenus as Xenophon's longstanding Theban guest friend and makes the choice between friend and city explicit. The prologue separates *Anabasis* 2.1.10 and 3.1.4–5 from the adapted letter and dialogue. Page 2 teaches personal and possessive pronouns, possessive adjectives, demonstratives, and adjective position, with practice and required exercises aligned to the new reading. Page 3 has two blocks on guest friendship and paid Greek military service after the Peloponnesian War, with the public-domain Achaemenid guard relief. The [revision migration](../../db/migrations/0031_refine_lesson_10_guest_friendship.sql) updates the database-backed version, and the bearded Xenophon appears in the lesson banner.

### Lesson 11 — The Question at Delphi

Xenophon travels to Delphi, carrying Socrates' advice but already inclined to go. He asks Apollo **which gods he should sacrifice and pray to** for a successful journey and safe return. According to [*Anabasis* 3.1.6](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0032%2C006%3A3), Apollo's response named the gods to whom he should sacrifice. Xenophon does not give the oracle's exact wording or identify those gods in this passage. Describe the journey and consultation vividly without inventing a purported quotation from the oracle.

**Language target:** Middle forms and common deponents, especially verbs of going, arriving, wanting, and consulting. Present-tense retelling can keep the beginner reading within the taught system.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 11 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-11.json) has an eight-paragraph present-tense reading, word study, five grammar sections, topic practice, required grammar exercises, a Delphi culture page, and a 30-question final quiz. Its banner shows Xenophon approaching Apollo’s sanctuary; the culture page uses [Albert Tournaire’s 1894 reconstruction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Delphi_by_Albert_Tournaire.jpg), labelled as a modern interpretation. The culture section draws on [UNESCO’s account of Delphi](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/393/) and [Herodotus’s Croesus narrative](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/herodotus-selections-part-i/) to explain the sanctuary’s reach across and beyond the Greek world. The reading labels its imagined route, dialogue, and thoughts as adaptations; it leaves the oracle’s wording and the named gods unspecified.

### Lesson 12 — The Question He Did Not Ask

Back in Athens, Xenophon reports the consultation. **In Xenophon's own account**, Socrates reproaches him: Xenophon asked how to make the journey successfully without first asking whether he should go. Socrates nevertheless tells him to obey Apollo's instruction. Xenophon performs the prescribed sacrifices and leaves toward Proxenus and Cyrus. This ending follows [*Anabasis* 3.1.7–8](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0032%2C006%3A3), while any expanded dialogue remains an adaptation. The final image should point forward to the army and the March of the Ten Thousand.

**Language target:** Middle meanings, datives, prepositions, and cumulative Module 1 review. The central contrast—*whether to go* versus *how to go well*—should remain understandable in simple Greek with support for any untaught construction.

**Authored lesson:** [Lesson 12 content](../../content/lessons/lesson-12.json) places a conversation image of Xenophon and Socrates in the banner and uses most of the Greek of *Anabasis* 3.1.7–8 in the reading, with blue glosses for untaught aorists, participles, optatives, and other constructions. The introduction identifies the two connective paragraphs and visual conversation as adaptations. Page 2 teaches the choice between going and staying, middle meanings, datives, prepositions, and cumulative review, with practice and required exercises. Page 3 ends at Sardis with a forward-looking image of the army and a final Module 1 quiz. The [publication migration](../../db/migrations/0033_publish_lesson_12.sql) replaces the website placeholder.

## Source and grammar rules for later authoring

1. Identify whether each scene is **directly reported in an ancient source**, **adapted from a reported episode**, or **reconstructed for the course**. Xenophon's account is a source, not independent proof that every reported conversation happened exactly as written.
2. Give women substantive actions and perspectives where the sources allow them; label invented dialogue clearly, especially in Lessons 7 and 8.
3. Prefer controlled present-tense narrative for Lessons 6–12. Gloss unfamiliar source vocabulary and grammar rather than making it an unannounced assessment target.
4. Follow the [grammar progression](grammar-progression-48-lessons.md) for Lessons 6–12. Lesson 7 now follows its planned I 4α allocation. Lessons 11–12 replace the older website placeholders rather than preserving those placeholder readings or activities.
5. Preserve the three-part ending: **Proxenus' invitation → Delphi → Socrates' response**. Module 2 begins with the expedition, not with a second retelling of the Delphic decision.

## Module II: ἀνδρεία — Courage and Leadership

**Scope:** Lessons 13–24. **Plan dated:** 11 October 2026. **Status:** Proposed readings and teaching sequence; lessons are not yet authored or published. These working titles supersede the earlier Module II planning titles. Website placeholders require a separate implementation pass.

### Narrative design and source use

The continuing story follows Xenophon from joining Cyrus's expedition to becoming one of the commanders responsible for its survival. The expedition moves forward through Sardis, the march across Asia Minor, the dispute at Tarsus, the revelation at Thapsacus, Cunaxa, the loss of the generals, and the first organized retreat. The module ends with the army learning to protect itself on the road; the mountains, winter march, and arrival at the sea remain available for later readings rather than being compressed into the review lesson.

Three substantial comparison readings interrupt the expedition at deliberate points: **Lesson 14, Memorabilia:** what qualifies someone to lead; **Lesson 17, Plato's Laches:** whether courage requires standing still and fighting; **Lesson 20, Hellenica:** the courage to address fellow citizens across a political division. They are separate narrated scenes, not invented conversations in Cyrus's camp. Brief opening and closing connections explain why each matters to the unfolding expedition. Lessons 13 and 23–24 also reconnect military leadership with Socrates' teaching.

The guiding question is **“What does a courageous leader owe to other people?”** Preparation, judgment, truthful persuasion, practical competence, and care for a community develop alongside battlefield courage. Epyaxa receives a full lesson as a consequential political actor. Ordinary soldiers' concerns, financial dependence, and civilian vulnerability matter throughout; the army is not presented as an uncomplicated band of heroes.

**Source boundary:** “Reported” means reported by the named ancient author, not independently established in every detail. All simplified Greek, expanded dialogue, transitions, and private thoughts must be identified as adaptations. Xenophon's presence at the Memorabilia conversations or Plato's dialogue is not assumed. No invented recollection is presented as an attested memory, and no scene has Xenophon reading a later work during the expedition. The Antisthenes elected general in Memorabilia 3.4 must not automatically be identified with the Socratic philosopher of the same name.

**Grammar basis:** Retain the established [48-lesson grammar progression](grammar-progression-48-lessons.md), using Athenaze Book I, 7α–12β. Its headings were checked against the supplied contents PDF, pages 3–5. This is a grammar allocation, not an adoption of the textbook's stories or explanations. The individual topics below expand that allocation into teaching sections. Each section needs explanation, reading examples, practice, and assessed exercises. Untaught forms in source extracts receive glosses and are not assessed as production targets. Lessons 13–20 primarily use controlled present-tense retelling; futures enter in Lesson 19 and aorist narrative in Lesson 21. The imperfect remains supported exposure until Lesson 25; subjunctives and full result-clause instruction are not Module II targets.

### Lesson 13 — Among the Soldiers at Sardis

**Reading:** Continue directly from Lesson 12. Xenophon enters the world of the assembled army, encounters Proxenus' contingent, and distinguishes friendship with a commander from responsibility for troops. A short companion passage introduces Socrates' image of a leader caring for the people entrusted to him. The reunion dialogue and Xenophon's observations are reconstructed; the gathering of contingents is source-based. Xenophon explicitly says he initially joined as neither general, captain, nor ordinary soldier.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.2.1–5](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.2) and [3.1.8–10](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+3.1); companion: [Memorabilia 3.2.1–4](https://monadnock.net/xenophon/mem3-2.html).

**Grammar — I, 7α:**
- Substantive adjectives: an adjective with the article can name a group, such as “the good” or “the brave.”
- Declension systems: identify dictionary forms, genitives, stems, gender, and endings; review first and second declensions before the third.
- Third-declension velar and dental stems: case formation and consonant changes, with controlled models such as φύλαξ and σῶμα.
- Reflexive pronouns: forms, agreement, and reference back to the subject; distinguish “himself” from “him.”

**Reading application:** Identify groups, guards, bodies, and equipment; contrast looking after oneself with looking after others. Courage begins with responsibility rather than a title.

### Lesson 14 — Who Is Fit to Lead?

**Reading:** A separate Socratic scene in Athens. Nicomachides, disappointed after the elections, displays his wounds and questions the choice of Antisthenes. Socrates asks whether organizing supplies, choosing skilled helpers, and obtaining cooperation also qualify someone to lead. Return to the expedition with an explicit comparison question: which of these abilities will an army need? The dialogue is adapted from Xenophon's account, not a meeting invented for Xenophon to witness.

**Sources:** Main reading: Xenophon, [Memorabilia 3.4.1–12](https://monadnock.net/xenophon/mem3-4.html). Short companion on preparation: [Memorabilia 3.1.1–7](https://monadnock.net/xenophon/mem3-1.html).

**Grammar — I, 7β:**
- Third-declension nasal stems: stems, nominative formation, and oblique cases.
- Third-declension labial and liquid stems: endings and relevant sound changes; compare them with Lesson 13's patterns.
- The adjective σώφρων, σῶφρον: masculine/feminine and neuter forms, declension, and agreement.
- Interrogative τίς, τί: forms and use as pronoun or adjective in direct questions.
- Indefinite τις, τι: forms, enclitic behavior, and the difference between “who?” and “someone.”

**Reading application:** Ask who can lead and what a leader needs; describe a prudent person. Experience and scars matter, but their sufficiency remains open to examination.

### Lesson 15 — An Army on the Move

**Reading:** Resume the march from Sardis through Phrygia. Select a few connected moments—crossing a river, reaching Celaenae, gathering further contingents, and attending the games at Peltae—rather than reproducing every stop. End with the pressure of overdue pay. Xenophon provides the itinerary and events; ordinary soldiers' conversations and the course's viewpoint character supply labeled connective reconstruction.

**Source:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.2.5–11](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.2).

**Grammar — I, 8α:**
- Present/progressive middle participles: formation with -όμενος, -ομένη, -όμενον and previously taught contract patterns where appropriate.
- Declension and agreement in gender, number, and case.
- Participles of familiar deponents, especially verbs of traveling and preparing; middle form does not always imply an English reflexive meaning.
- Attributive and substantival uses, followed by simple circumstantial uses supported by context.
- Ongoing action relative to the main verb: a present participle does not independently set calendar time.

**Reading application:** Follow people traveling, preparing, and assembling. Convert selected finite clauses into participial descriptions while keeping the same participants and meaning.

### Lesson 16 — Epyaxa and the Army's Pay

**Reading:** Epyaxa, wife of the Cilician ruler Syennesis, arrives while the troops are owed pay. Present the reported financial assistance, the payment of four months' wages, and the review she reportedly requested. Include the frightening effect of the display on her and on market traders. A brief continuation shows her role in persuading Syennesis to meet Cyrus. Her political action is central; her imagined words or motives must not masquerade as preserved testimony. Preserve Xenophon's distinction between report and assertion about the money.

**Source:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.2.12–18 and 1.2.25–27](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.2).

**Grammar — I, 8β:**
- Third-declension -ρ- stems, including family nouns such as πατήρ and μήτηρ; stem changes and case forms.
- The irregular nouns γυνή, γυναικός and χείρ, χειρός.
- πᾶς, πᾶσα, πᾶν: declension, agreement, and the distinction between “every,” “all,” and “whole” in context.
- Numbers: the chapter's assigned cardinal forms, agreement where inflected, and reading quantities.
- Time expressions: when, duration, and time within which; teach the relevant dative, accusative, and genitive constructions.

**Reading application:** Work with wages, months, days, the whole army, and people affected by its movements. Discuss the resources and negotiation that make military action possible.

### Lesson 17 — Must the Brave Always Stand Firm?

**Reading:** A separate adapted scene from Plato's Laches. Laches proposes the soldier who holds his position as an example of courage; Socrates tests the idea with people fighting while retreating and with courage beyond warfare. Close with a brief question about whether persistence needs judgment. This prepares readers to evaluate the coming refusal to march and, later, the retreat. Xenophon is not inserted into Plato's cast, and the excerpt does not resolve the dialogue's whole inquiry.

**Source:** Plato, [Laches 190e–192d](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/passage/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0059.tlg019.perseus-eng2:188-192/).

**Grammar — I, 9α:**
- Present/progressive active participles: formation of masculine, feminine, and neuter forms.
- Declension of -ων, -ουσα, -ον and recognition of the -οντ- stem; Lesson 18 generalizes the noun pattern.
- Agreement and identification of the noun or person described.
- Attributive, substantival, and simple circumstantial uses; contrast with the middle participles of Lesson 15.
- Simultaneous/ongoing action in context; a participle is not a separate finite verb.

**Reading application:** Distinguish those standing, pursuing, retreating, speaking, and enduring. Courage in illness, poverty, and public affairs broadens the question beyond soldiers.

### Lesson 18 — The Soldiers Refuse to March

**Reading:** At Tarsus, the soldiers suspect that Cyrus intends to attack the Great King. They refuse to proceed; Clearchus' attempt at force fails, and he turns to speeches and negotiation. Preserve the ambiguity of his conduct and the soldiers' legitimate concern about what they agreed to do. End with their decision to continue, while the true objective still awaits explicit announcement. “Obedience” is an ethical question here, not an automatic synonym for courage.

**Source:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.3.1–21](https://beta.perseus.tufts.edu/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0032.tlg006.perseus-eng2%3A1.3/).

**Grammar — I, 9β:**
- Third-declension nouns with -ντ- stems and their nominative/dative-plural changes.
- Vowel-stem nouns: πόλις and ἄστυ, with their case forms.
- Diphthong/vowel patterns: βασιλεύς and the irregular ναῦς and βοῦς.
- Genitive uses in the assigned chapter: distinguish possession and relationship, partitive expressions, and other chapter examples from time-within-which already taught.
- Article uses: substantivizing words or phrases and identifying groups or familiar persons; revisit adjective position and participles with the article.

**Reading application:** Track the city, the king, the soldiers' groups, and the available means of departure. Noun practice can include ships and cattle in explicitly labeled supplementary travel examples; every paradigm need not be forced into the source narrative.

### Lesson 19 — The King's War and Cyrus's Promises

**Reading:** At Thapsacus, Cyrus explicitly reveals that the expedition is against the Great King. The soldiers demand compensation; Cyrus promises money and continued pay, and they cross the Euphrates. A short second scene moves ahead to his pre-battle encouragement. Separate what Cyrus promises or predicts from what readers know will occur. Simplified future statements are course adaptations, not quotations from the speeches.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.4.11–18](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.4) and [1.7.3–9](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.7).

**Grammar — I, 10α:**
- Sigmatic futures: formation, active/middle endings, and stem changes before sigma.
- Asigmatic contract futures of verbs in -ίζω; forms and accents.
- Sigmatic futures of contract verbs: vowel lengthening and future formation; distinguish these from contract futures.
- Deponent futures and familiar verbs whose future has middle form.
- Read predictions, intentions, and promises in context; contrast present situation with future expectation.

**Reading application:** Sort promises about payment, return, and victory. Ask whether persuasive confidence gives the soldiers enough information to judge their risk.

### Lesson 20 — Courage to Address Fellow Citizens

**Reading:** A clearly dated comparison with Athens in 403 BCE, before the expedition of 401 BCE. After the fighting at Munychia, Cleocritus addresses the opposing citizens: shared worship, schooling, military service, family ties, and grief should matter more than obedience to the Thirty. Keep his appeal and the opposing officials' withdrawal distinct; do not invent immediate reconciliation or Xenophon's attendance. Return to Cyrus's expedition by comparing the purposes for which leaders ask people to act.

**Source:** Xenophon, [Hellenica 2.4.19–22](https://magalia.wiki/historiae/fulltext/xenophon-hellenica-text.html). Section numbers identify the passage within the linked text.

**Grammar — I, 10β:**
- Asigmatic contract futures of liquid/nasal stems: formation, contraction, and accents.
- εἶμι, “I shall go”: forms and contextual meaning; distinguish it from εἰμί, “I am.”
- Future participles expressing purpose: formation, agreement, and distinction from ongoing present participles.
- Impersonal verbs and expressions, including δεῖ and χρή with the infinitive where assigned.
- Review direct questions, interrogatives, and question words.

**Reading application:** Add short, labeled adapted statements of intention and purpose around the reported appeal. Ask who is speaking, why people approach one another, and what must happen next. Civic courage includes refusing destructive orders and recognizing shared obligations.

### Lesson 21 — Victory on the Field, Disaster for the Army

**Reading:** Tell a controlled, compact account of Cunaxa. The Greek troops drive back the opponents before them, while Cyrus attacks toward his brother and is killed. A short aftermath reveals why success on one part of the battlefield does not achieve the expedition's purpose. Distinguish the Greeks' immediate knowledge from the narrator's knowledge. Personal bravery and sound leadership are tested against consequences rather than assumed identical.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 1.8.17–29](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+1.8) and [2.1.1–4](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+2.1).

**Grammar — I, 11α:**
- Aorist indicative as past narrative: temporal augment, stems, and secondary endings.
- Thematic second aorist: active and middle forms.
- Aspect: present/progressive versus aorist presentation; an aorist is not inherently instantaneous.
- Thematic second-aorist active and middle participles: formation, agreement, and contextual relation to the main verb.
- Common second-aorist verbs: learn the assigned principal parts and recognize stem changes.

**Reading application:** Describe completed movements and discoveries using familiar second aorists, with present-tense dialogue where useful. First aorists or imperfects retained from source extracts are glossed, pending their formal teaching.

### Lesson 22 — Trust, Betrayal, and the Missing Generals

**Reading:** A brief bridge explains the negotiations after Cyrus's death. Clearchus and other commanders then enter Tissaphernes' meeting despite objections; the generals are seized and the captains outside are killed. A wounded survivor brings the news to camp. Focus on trust, dissent, and incomplete information rather than graphic injury. Do not collapse the generals' capture and their later fates into a single invented execution scene. The commander Socrates the Achaean is not Socrates the philosopher.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 2.5.25–35](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+2.5); the authoring background for later fates is [2.6](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Anab.+2.6).

**Grammar — I, 11β:**
- Thematic second aorists from unrelated stems: connect forms such as εἶπον and εἶδον with their dictionary verbs and principal parts.
- Active second-aorist imperative accents: the assigned exceptions and contrasts with familiar commands.
- Augment: syllabic and temporal formation, including vowel-initial verbs.
- Distinguish augmented indicative forms from unaugmented participles and imperatives; compound-verb augment receives its full treatment in Lesson 24.

**Reading application:** Track who went, saw, spoke, and brought news. Use short adapted commands in practice without attributing invented quotations to historical speakers.

### Lesson 23 — Xenophon Gets Up

**Reading:** Return to Xenophon personally. In the night of despair, he wakes from a troubling dream, questions his own inactivity, gathers Proxenus' captains, and begins helping the army rebuild its leadership. End with the choice of replacement commanders, including Xenophon in Proxenus' place. A brief companion with Socrates and Euthydemus distinguishes competent courage from ignorance of danger. Preserve Xenophon's uncertain interpretation of the dream; it is not proof of a divine promise. He becomes one of several leaders, not the sole savior of the army.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 3.1.11–25 and 3.1.32–47](https://cais-soas.com/CAIS/History/hakhamaneshian/xenophon/anabasis/xeno_anbs_book_3.htm); companion: [Memorabilia 4.6.10–11](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Xen.+Mem.+4.6).

**Grammar — I, 12α:**
- Sigmatic first aorist: stem formation, augment, and active/middle indicative endings.
- Consonant changes and vowel lengthening in first-aorist stems.
- Sigmatic first-aorist active and middle participles: formation and declension.
- Distinguish first from second aorist by formation; they share aorist aspect rather than expressing two different kinds of past time.

**Reading application:** Narrate calling, gathering, preparing, and speaking with first aorists where natural. Teach ἔστη and other athematic aorists only as glossed exposure; their full production belongs later. Link action to informed judgment, not to an absence of fear.

### Lesson 24 — Learning to Bring the Army Home

**Reading:** The reorganized army begins its retreat, suffers from enemy missiles, and discovers that courage alone will not solve a tactical problem. Xenophon and the other commanders identify useful skills and organize Rhodian slingers and a small cavalry force under Lycius. End with an army better equipped to continue, not already safely home. The short concluding comparison returns to Socrates' responsibility for those being led. This is the module's narrative payoff: the newcomer now contributes to collective survival.

**Sources:** Xenophon, [Anabasis 3.3.1–20](https://cais-soas.com/CAIS/History/hakhamaneshian/xenophon/anabasis/xeno_anbs_book_3.htm); companion: [Memorabilia 3.2.1–4](https://monadnock.net/xenophon/mem3-2.html).

**Grammar — I, 12β, plus cumulative review:**
- Asigmatic first aorists of liquid/nasal stems: formation, stem/vowel changes, and distinction from second aorists.
- Irregular sigmatic aorists: the chapter's assigned forms and principal parts.
- Augment in compound verbs: placement after the preverb and associated spelling changes.
- Cumulative noun and adjective systems, reflexives, interrogatives/indefinites, article and genitive uses, numbers, and time expressions.
- Cumulative present active/middle participles, future systems and purpose participles, and first/second aorists with their participles.

**Reading application and review:** Use the story for a connected reading assessment and short retelling; add focused exercises for each new aorist topic. Ask learners to compare Cyrus, Clearchus, Xenophon, and Cleocritus using the named readings. The cumulative review supplements all three new grammar topics rather than displacing them.

### Authoring and assessment priorities for Module II

Use one manageable core reading per lesson, normally six to eight short paragraphs or a comparable dialogue. Supplementary excerpts should be brief and serve an explicit comparison. Long source ranges above are selection pools, not assignments to translate the entire passage. Keep new vocabulary controlled and recycle it across the expedition. Do not force every grammar paradigm into every narrative; supplementary examples can cover forms absent from the source scene.

Keep source labels visible on the Reading page: author/work/passage, what is reported, what is adapted, and what the course has invented. The culture page should explain the current reading's setting, institutions, or ethical problem. For Epyaxa, distinguish her reported actions from conjectures about her feelings and motives; explain the harm or fear experienced by civilians as well as the army's perspective.

Each listed grammar topic needs its own teaching section, worked examples, formative practice, and assessed application. Use interpretation questions as well as morphology: who is acting, which people a participle describes, whether an action is ongoing or viewed as a whole, and whether a future is a promise, purpose, or prediction. Source-comparison questions should be answerable from the supplied readings, without requiring untaught Greek constructions.
