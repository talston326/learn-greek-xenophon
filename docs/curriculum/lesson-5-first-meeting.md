## Reading


ὁ Ξενοφῶν διὰ στενωποῦ βαδίζει. ἐθέλει εἰς τὴν ἀγορὰν σπεύδειν· ἄρτον γὰρ ἐθέλει λαμβάνειν. ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ οἱ ἔμποροι λέγουσι καὶ οἱ ἄνθρωποι ἀκούουσιν. οἱ μὲν ἄρτον φέρουσιν, οἱ δὲ οἶνον. ὁ δὲ Ξενοφῶν λέγει· «καλὸν μὲν τὸ μανθάνειν, ἀγαθὸς δὲ καὶ ὁ ἄρτος.»



Xenophon walks through a narrow lane. He wants to hurry to the marketplace, for he wants to get bread. In the marketplace the merchants speak and the people listen. Some carry bread, others wine. Xenophon says, “Learning is fine, but bread is good too.”



ἐν δὲ τῷ στενωπῷ ὁ Σωκράτης τὸν Ξενοφῶντα βλέπει. τὴν βακτηρίαν ἐκτείνει καὶ κωλύει τὸν νεανίαν. ὁ Ξενοφῶν μένει καὶ θαυμάζει. ἡ μὲν βακτηρία μακρά ἐστιν, ὁ δὲ στενωπὸς στενός. «ὦ φίλε,» λέγει ὁ Ξενοφῶν, «τί ποιεῖς; ἐθέλω βαδίζειν, ἀλλὰ σὺ κωλύεις.»



In the lane Socrates sees Xenophon. He stretches out his staff and blocks the young man. Xenophon stops and is surprised. The staff is long, and the lane is narrow. “My friend,” says Xenophon, “what are you doing? I want to walk, but you are blocking the way.”



ὁ δὲ Σωκράτης λέγει· «μὴ σπεῦδε· μένε καὶ ἄκουε. ποῦ οἱ ἄνθρωποι ἄρτον λαμβάνουσιν;» ὁ Ξενοφῶν λέγει· «ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ. ἐκεῖ ἡ ἀρτοπῶλις ἄρτον παρασκευάζει. καλὸν ἄρτον ἔχει.» ὁ δὲ Σωκράτης πάλιν λέγει· «ποῦ δὲ οἶνον; ποῦ δὲ ὑποδήματα;» ὁ Ξενοφῶν ταχέως λέγει· «καὶ ταῦτα ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ· ἐγὼ γὰρ τὴν ἀγορὰν γιγνώσκω.»



Socrates says, “Do not hurry. Stay and listen. Where do people get bread?” Xenophon says, “In the marketplace. The bread seller prepares bread there. She has good bread.” Socrates asks again, “And where do they get wine? And shoes?” Xenophon replies quickly, “Those too are in the marketplace, for I know the marketplace.”



ἡ δὲ ἀρτοπῶλις παρὰ τῇ θύρᾳ ἀκούει καὶ λέγει· «καλῶς λέγεις, ὦ νεανία· δεῦρο, λάμβανε ἄρτον.» ὁ δὲ Σωκράτης οὐ τὴν βακτηρίαν καταβάλλει, ἀλλὰ πάλιν λέγει· «ποῦ δὲ καλοὶ καὶ ἀγαθοὶ γίγνονται οἱ ἄνθρωποι;» ὁ Ξενοφῶν οὐκέτι ταχέως λέγει. βλέπει μὲν τὴν ἀγορὰν, βλέπει δὲ τὴν βακτηρίαν· ἔπειτα σιγᾷ.



The bread seller listens beside the door and says, “You speak well, young man. Come here; take some bread.” But Socrates does not lower his staff. Instead he asks again, “And where do people become good and honorable?” Xenophon no longer replies quickly. He looks at the marketplace, then at the staff; then he falls silent.



ὁ Σωκράτης λέγει· «ἄρτον μὲν γιγνώσκεις, οἶνον δὲ γιγνώσκεις· σοφίαν δὲ ζητεῖς;» ὁ Ξενοφῶν λέγει· «ἐθέλω μανθάνειν, ὦ Σώκρατες, ἀλλὰ νῦν οὐκ οἶδα.» ἡ δὲ ἀρτοπῶλις λέγει· «ἄρτον ἔχω· σοφίαν δὲ οὐκ ἔχω. τὸν ἄρτον λάμβανε, τοῦ δὲ Σωκράτους ἄκουε.» ὁ Σωκράτης μειδιᾷ.



Socrates says, “You know bread, and you know wine; but do you seek wisdom?” Xenophon says, “I want to learn, Socrates, but at present I do not know.” The bread seller says, “I have bread; I do not have wisdom. Take the bread, and listen to Socrates.” Socrates smiles.



οἱ δὲ φίλοι τοῦ Σωκράτους προσέρχονται καὶ μένουσιν. ὁ Σωκράτης λέγει· «ἀκούετε, ὦ φίλοι· μὴ σπεύδετε. ὁ νεανίας ἐθέλει μανθάνειν.» οἱ φίλοι τὸν Ξενοφῶντα βλέπουσι καὶ λέγουσιν· «φέρε τὸν ἄρτον καὶ βάδιζε.» ὁ δὲ Ξενοφῶν λέγει· «ἆρα οἱ φίλοι σοφίαν ἐθέλουσι μανθάνειν ἢ ἄρτον ἐσθίειν;»



Socrates’ friends approach and stop. Socrates says, “Listen, friends; do not hurry. The young man wants to learn.” The friends look at Xenophon and say, “Bring the bread and walk along.” Xenophon says, “Do the friends want to learn wisdom or eat bread?”



ὁ Σωκράτης τὴν βακτηρίαν καταβάλλει καὶ λέγει· «ἕπου τοίνυν καὶ μάνθανε.» ὁ δὲ Ξενοφῶν λαμβάνει τὸν ἄρτον καὶ σὺν τῷ Σωκράτει βαδίζει. οἱ φίλοι βαδίζουσι καὶ διαλέγονται. ἡ δὲ ἀρτοπῶλις λέγει· «μανθάνετε, ὦ φίλοι· ἀλλὰ καὶ τὸν ἄρτον φέρετε.» ὁ Ξενοφῶν τὸν ἄρτον φέρει· ἤδη δὲ ἐθέλει οὐ μόνον ἐσθίειν ἀλλὰ καὶ μανθάνειν.



Socrates lowers his staff and says, “Then follow me and learn.” Xenophon takes the bread and walks with Socrates. The friends walk and converse. The bread seller says, “Learn, friends—but take the bread along too.” Xenophon carries the bread; now he wants not only to eat but also to learn.

### 1. Who Is Speaking? Singular Verb Review


An indicative verb makes a statement or asks a question. In the present active of regular -ω verbs, -ω marks I, -εις marks you (one person), and -ει marks he, she, or it. Greek often leaves out a subject pronoun because the ending supplies the person.

In the dialogue, λέγω means I say, λέγεις means you say, and λέγει means he or she says. The bread seller speaks too: her verb has the same third-person ending as Socrates’ verb. Gender does not change this ending.

Present forms can be translated I carry, I am carrying, or I do carry as the context requires. Identify the ending before guessing the speaker.



### 2. From One Person to Several: -ουσι(ν)


The third-person plural present active ending is -ουσι(ν): they. Compare λέγει (he or she speaks) with λέγουσι or λέγουσιν (they speak). Add the ending to the present stem: λεγ- + ουσι → λέγουσι.

The final ν is called movable nu. It is commonly used before a vowel or at a pause; it can also occur before a consonant. Both forms have the same meaning and person. Do not confuse movable nu with a new tense or a noun ending.

The reading supplies οἱ φίλοι, οἱ ἔμποροι, and οἱ ἄνθρωποι as plural subjects. Recognize these groups for comprehension; full plural noun and article paradigms belong to Lesson 6.

Keep the regular verb’s recessive accent as far back as permitted. Compare λαμβάνει with λαμβάνουσιν and μανθάνει with μανθάνουσιν.



### 3. Stay and Listen: Commands to One or More


An imperative tells someone to do something. For the regular present active, use stem + -ε to address one person, and stem + -ετε to address more than one. There is normally no expressed subject.

Socrates says μένε καὶ ἄκουε to Xenophon: stay and listen. He says ἀκούετε to his friends: listen, all of you. A vocative may identify the audience: ὦ φίλοι is supplied as friends!.

Use μή with these commands to say do not: μὴ σπεῦδε to one person; μὴ σπεύδετε to several. A statement normally uses οὐ instead: οὐ σπεύδει, he does not hurry.

The -ετε form can also be an indicative meaning you (plural) do something. Here the direct appeal to the listeners makes it a command; the complete indicative paradigm comes in Lesson 7.

The source’s ἕπου (follow!) is a middle imperative. Recognize the supplied expression here; do not build a middle-voice paradigm yet. Regular μάνθανε is our model for the second half of the invitation.



### 4. Wanting to Learn: Present Active Infinitives


An infinitive names an action without marking a person or number. In these regular verbs its ending is -ειν: μανθάνειν, to learn; φέρειν, to carry. It is not an imperative.

After ἐθέλω, the infinitive completes the idea: ἐθέλω μανθάνειν means I want to learn. Change the finite verb for the subject; leave the infinitive unchanged: ἐθέλει μανθάνειν, he or she wants to learn; ἐθέλουσι μανθάνειν, they want to learn.

In these examples the person wanting and the person doing the infinitive’s action are the same. We are not yet studying infinitives with a separate accusative subject or complex indirect speech.

The -ειν ending has a long diphthong. Compare μάνθανε, learn!, with μανθάνειν, to learn: the accent cannot remain on the antepenult before the long final syllable.

τὸ μανθάνειν in the first paragraph means learning. That article-plus-infinitive construction is supplied exposure only, not an additional assessed objective.



### 5. Small Words: Proclitics and Negatives


A proclitic normally has no accent of its own and leans forward onto the following word. Review these ten: ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ; ἐν, εἰς, ἐκ (ἐξ); εἰ, ὡς; οὐ (οὐκ, οὐχ). The unfamiliar items are recognition examples, not a requirement to use new clause constructions.

Only the four article forms listed above are proclitic. Do not remove accents from other forms: τό, τόν, τῷ, and τῆς retain their own accents.

Use οὐ before a consonant, οὐκ before a vowel with smooth breathing, and οὐχ before a vowel with rough breathing: οὐ λέγει, οὐκ ἀκούει, οὐχ ὁ φίλος. At the end of a clause or when standing alone, the negative can be accented οὔ.

μή, the negative used with our commands, is not a proclitic. Its acute becomes grave before another word in continuous text: μὴ σπεῦδε. This reviews the accent rule from Lesson 4.

Keep breathing marks: ὁ and οἱ have rough breathing, while οὐ and ἐν have smooth breathing. Lack of an accent does not mean lack of a breathing mark.
