# Grammar progression: 48 lessons

**Project:** Learn Greek with Xenophon  
**Prepared:** 14 September 2026  
**Status:** Proposed lesson allocation for review; not yet implemented  
**Scope confirmed by Tom:** Athenaze Books I and II  
**Edition used for this draft:** English third edition (Balme, Lawall, and Morwood). Edition assumed; the two-book scope was explicitly confirmed.

## Governing decision

The grammar sequence follows Athenaze. Reading subjects, characters, titles, and illustrations may change without changing that sequence. Grammar assignments belong to stable lesson numbers, not to story titles.

This document proposes how to fit the textbook sequence into our 48 lessons. The allocation is our curriculum design, not a claim that Oxford publishes a 48-lesson syllabus. Approval of this document would establish the reference for subsequent lesson authoring and alignment work.

The existing app outline is not a reliable record of this intended progression. This document does not automatically change the app, its database, or existing student assessments.

## Allocation and pacing

- Lessons 1–32: Book I, one lesson for each chapter half, α then β.
- Lessons 33–48: Book II, one lesson per chapter except Chapters 21 and 25, which each receive two lessons.
- Alphabet, pronunciation, breathings, and keyboard preparation remain in Unit 0, outside the 48 numbered lessons.
- A lesson is a learning unit, not necessarily one class meeting. Book II units will generally require more time and practice. Chapters 18–20, 23, and 26–28 especially need multiple grammar sections and practice sessions within their assigned lesson.
- Review is cumulative within every lesson. Reviews at Lessons 12, 24, 36, and 48 supplement their assigned content; they do not replace it or push the next topic forward.

This allocation preserves the textbook's order while giving beginners smaller steps. It accelerates the allocation in Book II. If equal workload per lesson becomes a requirement, the 48-unit limit should be reconsidered rather than silently dropping grammar topics.

## How to read the tables

“Focus” is a compact orientation, not an exhaustive list of subtopics. **The scope of each row includes all formal grammar sections in the cited chapter or chapter half**, including associated accent rules, irregular forms, and syntax. When writing an individual lesson, expand that reference into an objective checklist using the stated edition. Do not use the short focus label as a reason to omit a subsection.

## What this means for the four existing readings

The readings can retain their current subjects. Their grammar pages, glosses, and assessments need an alignment pass before we describe the course as following this plan.

| Existing lesson | Action needed under this proposal |
|---|---|
| 1 — Xenophon at Home | Audit the existing grammar and exercises against its assigned reference. Mark additional reading forms as supported exposure. |
| 2 — The Household of Xenophon | Keep the household story. Reconcile its broader current grammar coverage with the assigned sequence; do not treat prior exposure as proof of mastery. |
| 3 — The Education of Xenophon | Its current objectives range ahead of its proposed reference. Rework the formal objectives and tests while preserving useful reading material. |
| 4 — Gryllus Rides to War | Build the missing grammar and final pages around its assigned case-and-agreement work. Retain the departure story. |

For Lesson 4, useful original examples are the father as subject, the helmet as object, a gift or preparation for Gryllus, and equipment belonging to the father. These give the case work a practical setting. The presence of irregular nouns, an infinitive, or a participle in the narrative does not authorize adding their complete paradigms to this early lesson's assessment.

The case function and the full declension of a particular noun are separate learning decisions. A learner can understand a glossed form in a sentence before being expected to generate its entire paradigm.

## Rules for changing a reading

1. Keep the assigned textbook reference and formal grammar objectives fixed unless Tom explicitly changes the curriculum.
2. Write the new story primarily with grammar already taught plus the current lesson's targets.
3. Label every additional construction as supported exposure. Supply a clear gloss or reading note; do not test independent production of that construction yet.
4. Distinguish required new vocabulary, review vocabulary, and words supplied only for comprehension. Story revisions may change vocabulary without moving grammar objectives.
5. Preserve independently written explanations and exercises. Borrow the sequence, not Athenaze's prose, stories, or exercise banks.
6. After a revision, check the Greek, translation, glosses, vocabulary, grammar examples, exercises, and quiz together.
7. Record the reading revision separately from any approved change to this grammar map.

## Authoring requirements for each lesson

Every completed lesson should contain:

- **Reading:** the current story, translation, vocabulary, and support for unfamiliar forms.
- **Language study:** objectives expanded from the assigned reference, explanations, paradigms as appropriate, guided examples, and practice.
- **Final page:** cultural context relevant to the current story, cumulative review, and an assessment limited to taught objectives.

At authoring time, maintain a checklist with these fields: lesson number; textbook reference; objective; first formal teaching; prior supported appearances; practice coverage; assessment coverage; completion status. This makes it possible to change a story without losing the progression.

A lesson is complete only when all three pages are available and its published content matches the reviewed version. For this application, checking the repository alone is insufficient: verify the database-backed lesson served by the live API as well.
