Module II: Ἀνδρεία —
Courage and Leadership

In this module, we turn to ἀνδρεία, courage. In Xenophon, courage is never simply recklessness or love of danger. It is the ability to act with steadiness when fear, hardship, confusion, or danger might otherwise take command. Courage requires endurance, but it also requires judgment. The courageous person must know when to stand firm, when to obey, when to lead, and when restraint is wiser than display.

Xenophon gives us some of the most vivid leadership scenes in Greek literature. In the Anabasis, Greek soldiers find themselves stranded deep in hostile territory after the death of Cyrus. The army survives not because danger disappears, but because leaders emerge who can speak clearly, organize action, and sustain morale. Xenophon's own role in that story gives students a powerful example of courage under pressure.

Lessons 13–24 follow the expedition from Sardis through Cunaxa to the army's first organized retreat. Separate scenes from the Memorabilia, Plato's Laches, and the Hellenica ask what makes a leader useful, whether courage always means standing firm, and what fellow citizens owe one another. The central question is: what does a courageous leader owe other people? Adapted readings introduce third-declension forms, participles, futures, and aorists in the course's teaching sequence.

Texts and Themes

Primary Xenophon Focus

  • Anabasis: the expedition's continuing storyline
  • Memorabilia: preparation, competence, and responsibility
  • Hellenica 2.4: Cleocritus's appeal to fellow citizens
  • Plato, Laches: questioning the meaning of courage

Themes

  • Courage under pressure
  • Leadership and persuasion
  • Endurance during hardship
  • Fear, discipline, and decision-making
  • The relationship between commander and community
